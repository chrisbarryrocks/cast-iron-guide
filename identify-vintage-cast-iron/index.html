How to Identify Vintage Cast Iron: Griswold, Wagner, Lodge, and Unmarked Pans

Updated 2026-10-29 · 5 min read

You found an old cast iron skillet at an estate sale or in a family kitchen. Who made it? Is it old? Is it worth anything? Identifying vintage cast iron is part detective work and part pattern recognition. This guide covers the main clues to look for and how to read them, along with the most common makers you're likely to find in the US.

A note on accuracy: cast iron collecting has a deep body of specialist knowledge, and precise dating of specific logos and patterns is best done with collector references and communities. This guide teaches you what to look for so you can research confidently.

Start with the bottom

Clean the bottom of the pan enough to see markings. Heavy buildup can hide everything; if needed, strip the pan first using a collector-safe method such as lye or electrolysis. See our restoration guide.

Then look for:

  1. A maker's name or logo
  2. A size number, often a large number near the handle or at the center (for example, 3, 5, 8, 10, 12)
  3. A pattern or catalog number, often a number with a letter, like "704 E"
  4. A heat ring, a raised ring around the outer edge of the bottom
  5. Location markings, such as a city name
  6. "Made in USA" or other country markings
  7. Gate marks, a raised line or scar across the bottom

Size numbers

Many vintage skillets carry a number that indicates their size. The numbers historically corresponded to the openings, or "eyes," on wood-burning cook stoves, not directly to inches. A #8 skillet is a common size, typically around 10½ inches across the top rim, though dimensions vary somewhat between makers and eras.

Size numbers are useful because they're consistent enough to help you compare a pan to reference listings.

Heat rings

A heat ring is a raised ring on the bottom of a skillet, near the edge. It originally helped pans sit in the opening of a wood stove. Heat rings appear on many older pans from several manufacturers. Whether a pan has a heat ring, and its style (some are notched, some are set in from the edge), can help collectors date pans. Heat rings alone don't identify a maker.

Gate marks

A gate mark is a long raised scar across the bottom of a pan, left where molten iron entered the mold in older casting methods. Gate marks are generally a sign of an older pan, often considered to predate the early 20th century, since later casting processes moved the gate. If your pan has a gate mark, it's likely quite old, though gate-marked pans are usually unmarked and harder to attribute to a maker.

The major American makers

Griswold

Griswold Manufacturing operated in Erie, Pennsylvania, and is probably the most famous name in American vintage cast iron. Griswold pans are known for smooth, machined cooking surfaces and relatively light weight.

Key identifying features:

  • The word "ERIE" on some early pieces, before the Griswold name was used prominently
  • The Griswold cross logo, a cross inside a circle with "Griswold" and "Erie, PA" around it, found in different sizes and styles
  • Collectors commonly distinguish a large block logo and a small block logo, among other variants, and use these to help date pans
  • Pattern numbers on the bottom
  • Size number usually near the handle

Griswold went through ownership changes in the mid-20th century, and its brand was eventually owned by the same parent company as Wagner. Later pieces can differ in quality and markings. Collector references are the best way to pin down exact eras.

Wagner

Wagner Manufacturing was based in Sidney, Ohio, and made large quantities of cast iron for many decades. Wagner pans are also known for smooth surfaces and good quality, and they are often more affordable than Griswold on the vintage market.

Identifying features:

  • "Wagner" or "Wagner Ware" markings, often with "Sidney -O-"
  • Various logo styles, including stylized script and block lettering
  • Size numbers and pattern numbers
  • Some pieces carry a catalog number with a letter suffix

Lodge

Lodge, founded in South Pittsburg, Tennessee, in 1896, is still making cast iron today. Older Lodge pieces were often unmarked or marked only with a size number and a notch in the heat ring, which makes them harder to identify than Griswold or Wagner. Later Lodge pans have the Lodge name cast into the bottom. Modern Lodge is pre-seasoned with vegetable oil and has a textured surface.

Other names you'll encounter

  • Birmingham Stove & Range (BSR) of Alabama, often with "Century" or "Red Mountain" series names
  • Vollrath, a Wisconsin company known for many products including some cast iron
  • Favorite Piqua Ware from Piqua, Ohio
  • Martin Stove & Range of Florence, Alabama
  • Wapak (Wapakoneta, Ohio), sometimes with an "Indian head" logo
  • Sears, Montgomery Ward, and other store brands, often made by other foundries

Unmarked pans

Many vintage pans have no maker's name. That doesn't mean they're low quality. Several manufacturers sold unmarked lines, often through department stores. Clues for unmarked pans:

  • Size number style and placement
  • Heat ring style, including notches
  • Handle shape, including the shape of the hanging hole and any ridge or thumb rest
  • Pour spout shape
  • Thickness and weight
  • Font style on any letters or numbers

Collectors frequently identify unmarked pans by comparing these features with known examples. Online collector communities are very helpful for this.

Signs a pan is probably newer

  • Pebbly, textured cooking surface (unless someone sanded it smooth)
  • Modern country-of-origin markings like "Made in China" or "Taiwan"
  • Very heavy weight for its size (not always conclusive)
  • Modern brand logos and printed care instructions cast in
  • Factory pre-seasoning that looks uniformly black and slightly textured

A smooth surface doesn't prove a pan is vintage. Some modern makers mill their pans smooth, and some owners sand modern pans.

Reproductions and fakes

Because Griswold pans are valuable, reproductions exist. Warning signs include rough or grainy surface texture, fuzzy or poorly defined lettering, logos that don't match known styles, and unusual weights. If you're buying a pricey piece, compare it carefully with documented examples, or ask a collector community.

What affects value

  • Maker and logo style
  • Rarity of the size or pattern; some sizes were made in smaller quantities
  • Condition: cracks, warping, and pitting reduce value significantly
  • Surface: smooth, undamaged cooking surfaces are prized
  • Demand, which varies over time

Common sizes from common makers, like a #8 skillet, are often worth modest amounts but are excellent cooking pans. Rare sizes, early logos, and unusual items can be worth much more. Check sold listings on marketplaces like eBay, rather than asking prices, to get a realistic sense of current value.

Quick checklist

Clue Where to look What it tells you
Maker name / logo Bottom center Manufacturer, sometimes era
Size number Bottom near handle or center Size
Pattern number Bottom Specific model
Heat ring Bottom outer edge Older style; notches can hint at maker
Gate mark Long scar across bottom Usually very old
City name Bottom near logo Foundry location
Surface finish Cooking surface Machined smooth = often older

Cooking with vintage pieces

Once you've identified a pan, the best thing you can do is use it. Vintage cast iron, once restored and seasoned, cooks beautifully. Start with our seasoning guide and enjoy a pan with a history.
