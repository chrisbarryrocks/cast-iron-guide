How to Season a Carbon Steel Pan: Stovetop and Oven Methods

Updated 2026-10-29 · 5 min read

Carbon steel pans are prized for being light, responsive, and very slick once seasoned. But new owners often find that seasoning carbon steel is more finicky than seasoning cast iron. The surface is smooth, so seasoning has less texture to hold onto, and the pan often looks blotchy for weeks. Here's how to get it right.

Step 1: Remove the factory coating

Most new carbon steel pans arrive with a protective coating to stop rust during shipping. It's usually either:

  • Beeswax or a similar wax (common on French pans like de Buyer)
  • A clear lacquer or varnish
  • Mineral oil or light grease
  • Factory pre-seasoning (some brands, like Lodge, ship carbon steel pre-seasoned)

Always check the manufacturer's instructions first, since methods differ by brand.

Wax coatings: Many manufacturers recommend rinsing the pan with very hot water to melt the wax, then washing with soap. Some suggest warming the pan in the oven briefly first so the wax softens, then wiping it off and washing.

Lacquer or varnish: These usually need more work. Common approaches are scrubbing with hot soapy water and a stiff scrubber, or boiling water in the pan and then scrubbing. Some manufacturers specify a particular method, such as boiling water with potato peels or using a specific solvent. Follow the brand's instructions.

Pre-seasoned pans: Wash with warm water and a little soap and you're ready to cook, though a few more coats help.

After removing the coating, dry the pan completely and heat it on the stove to drive off moisture.

Step 2: Choose your seasoning method

Carbon steel can be seasoned on the stovetop or in the oven. Many owners find the stovetop method faster and more natural for carbon steel's thin build. The oven gives more even coverage including the handle and exterior.

Stovetop method

  1. Heat the clean, dry pan over medium-high heat until it's hot, a few minutes.
  2. Add a few drops of oil, such as vegetable, canola, grapeseed, or a commercial seasoning product.
  3. Wipe it over the entire interior with a folded paper towel held in tongs (the pan is hot).
  4. Wipe off excess until the surface looks almost dry.
  5. Keep heating until the oil smokes and then the smoking subsides. The steel will start to darken, often to a bronze or brown color first.
  6. Let the pan cool slightly, then repeat steps 2–5 several times. Many cooks do five to ten rounds.

You'll see the color shift as you go: silver to gold, to bronze, to brown, and eventually toward black with more use. Uneven color at this stage is normal.

Tip: Tilt and move the pan over the burner to heat the sides and corners. The center of a carbon steel pan heats fastest, so the middle often darkens first.

Oven method

  1. Preheat the oven to 450–500°F.
  2. Apply a very thin layer of oil to all surfaces of the warm, dry pan.
  3. Wipe until the pan looks dry.
  4. Place it upside down in the oven for about an hour.
  5. Let it cool in the oven.
  6. Repeat three to five times.

Handle note: Most carbon steel handles are steel and oven-safe. If your pan has a wooden, silicone, or coated handle, check the manufacturer's maximum temperature before oven seasoning, or use the stovetop method.

Step 3: Cook-in seasoning

Whatever method you start with, the fastest way to build durable seasoning on carbon steel is to cook fatty foods:

  • Fry potato peels or sliced potatoes in oil. A classic technique recommended by several French pan makers. Fry, discard, wipe out, and repeat if you like.
  • Shallow-fry or sear with plenty of oil.
  • Cook bacon.

Avoid acidic foods, such as tomato sauce, wine reductions, and citrus, for the first several weeks.

What normal looks like

New carbon steel seasoning often looks:

  • Blotchy or spotty, with bronze, brown, and black areas
  • Darker in the center, where heat is concentrated
  • Rainbow or bluish in places, which is heat tint on the steel
  • Lighter on the sides, which see less heat and fat

All of that is normal and cosmetic. Over months, the pan typically becomes darker and more uniform. Performance often improves long before appearance does.

Troubleshooting

Seasoning flakes off early

Common on carbon steel because the smooth surface gives seasoning less grip. Usually caused by thick coats, residual factory coating, or acidic cooking. Scrub the flaky area, re-season with very thin coats, and cook fatty foods.

Sticky surface

Too much oil, not enough heat. Heat the pan on the stove until it smokes and the surface turns dry. If it's very sticky, scrub and start the layer again more thinly.

Rust spots

Carbon steel rusts like cast iron. Scrub the spot with steel wool, dry, and re-season the area. See how to remove rust from cast iron; the method is the same.

Food sticks

New seasoning plus too little fat or heat. Preheat properly, use enough fat, and let food release before moving it. More in why food sticks to cast iron, which applies equally to carbon steel.

The pan warped

Carbon steel can warp, especially thinner pans used on very high heat on a burner smaller than the pan, or when a hot pan is rinsed in cold water. Preheat gradually, match the burner size to the pan, and let the pan cool before washing. Mild warping can sometimes be corrected, but prevention is easier.

Daily care

  • Wash while warm with hot water and a brush; a little soap is fine
  • Dry immediately, then heat on the burner for a minute
  • Wipe on a few drops of oil
  • Never put it in the dishwasher
  • Don't soak it

Full details in how to clean cast iron.

Best oils for carbon steel

The same oils that work on cast iron work on carbon steel: vegetable, canola, grapeseed, sunflower, shortening, or commercial seasoning blends. Many cooks avoid flaxseed on carbon steel because flaking is already a common early issue. See best oil for seasoning cast iron.

How long until it's truly nonstick?

With good initial seasoning and regular use, many cooks find carbon steel handles eggs well within a few weeks. Very slick, glassy seasoning typically develops over a few months of frequent cooking.
