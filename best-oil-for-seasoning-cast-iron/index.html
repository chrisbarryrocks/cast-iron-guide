The Best Oil for Seasoning Cast Iron (and Why It Matters Less Than You Think)

Updated 2026-10-29 · 5 min read

Ask ten cast iron owners for the best seasoning oil and you'll get ten answers, often delivered with total conviction. The truth is less dramatic: most common cooking fats will season cast iron well if you apply them thinly and bake them hot. The differences between oils are real but small compared to technique.

That said, oil choice does affect how hard, durable, and even the finish turns out, and a few choices cause more problems than they solve. Here's how the common options compare.

What makes an oil good for seasoning

Seasoning forms when fats oxidize and polymerize into a hard film under heat. A few factors matter:

  • Degree of unsaturation. Oils with more unsaturated fat, especially polyunsaturated fat, tend to polymerize more readily. This is why "drying oils" like flaxseed form films quickly, and why highly saturated fats like coconut oil form softer layers.
  • Smoke point. You'll bake above it either way, but oils with very high smoke points may need hotter ovens to cure fully.
  • Cost and availability. You'll use small amounts, but you want something you have on hand for regular maintenance.
  • Flavor and rancidity. Excess unpolymerized oil can go rancid in storage, regardless of the type. Thin application prevents this.

The case for plain vegetable oil or shortening

If you want one answer, use vegetable oil, canola oil, or vegetable shortening. They're inexpensive, widely available, polymerize well at 450–500°F, and produce a durable finish. Lodge's own factory seasoning uses vegetable oil, and Lodge recommends vegetable oil, melted shortening, or canola oil on its care pages.

Shortening is especially easy to apply thinly because it's solid at room temperature. Rub a tiny amount on with a paper towel and buff it off.

The flaxseed oil debate

Flaxseed oil became popular after a widely shared blog post around 2010 argued that, as a drying oil, it forms the hardest seasoning. It does polymerize into a hard, attractive finish, and some people love it.

The problem is that many users report flaxseed seasoning flaking after a few months, especially with high-heat cooking or when applied in many coats. A hard film is not necessarily a tough one. It can be brittle. Flaxseed oil is also more expensive and must be stored cold to avoid going rancid.

If you've had flaking with flaxseed, switching to an ordinary oil usually solves it. If you want to try flaxseed, apply it extremely thin and use fewer coats.

Lard, bacon grease, and other animal fats

Traditional cooks seasoned pans with lard, and it works. Animal fats are more saturated, so the layer they form is softer than one from vegetable oils. They're excellent for cooking-in seasoning, since frying bacon in a new pan does wonders, but for oven seasoning coats from bare metal, vegetable oil generally gives a harder result.

Leftover bacon grease contains salt and food particles, so strain it if you plan to use it for maintenance.

Avocado and other high smoke point oils

Avocado oil has one of the highest smoke points of common oils. That's great for cooking but means you need a hotter oven to fully polymerize it. If you season with avocado oil at 450°F and get a sticky result, try 500°F or a longer bake.

Olive oil

Extra-virgin olive oil has a lower smoke point and can smoke heavily in a hot oven. It works for maintenance and cooking-in seasoning but is not ideal for building oven coats on bare iron. If it's what you have, use light or refined olive oil, which tolerates heat better.

Coconut oil and butter

Coconut oil is mostly saturated fat and forms a softer, less durable film. It's not harmful, just less effective. Butter contains milk solids and water that burn, so it's a poor choice for oven seasoning, though it's perfectly fine for cooking in a seasoned pan.

Commercial seasoning products

Several companies sell seasoning sticks, sprays, and pastes, often blends of oils and waxes such as beeswax. They're convenient and work well, especially for daily maintenance. They aren't necessary, but if you like the convenience, they're a reasonable choice. Avoid products not intended for food contact.

Technique beats oil every time

Whatever you choose, these factors matter more than the oil itself:

  1. Apply a very thin layer, then wipe it nearly all off. Thick coats cause sticky, blotchy, or flaking seasoning with any oil.
  2. Bake above the smoke point, typically 450–500°F, for about an hour.
  3. Bake upside down so excess oil doesn't pool.
  4. Build multiple thin coats rather than one thick one.
  5. Cook in the pan regularly. Everyday cooking adds seasoning layers no matter what oil you started with.

Can you mix oils?

Yes. Seasoning built with one oil can be maintained with another. You don't need to strip a pan to switch oils. If you're troubleshooting flaking, though, removing the loose layers before switching helps the new seasoning bond.

What about the "rancid oil" concern?

Unpolymerized oil left on a pan can go rancid, giving a stale smell. Properly cured seasoning doesn't go rancid because it's no longer oil in the usual sense. If your stored pan smells off, you're leaving too much oil after maintenance. Wash it, dry it, and use less next time. Storing in a dry place with good airflow also helps.

Our recommendations

  • Best all-around: Vegetable oil, canola oil, or shortening.
  • Best for daily maintenance: Whatever neutral oil you cook with, applied in a few drops.
  • Best for cooking-in seasoning: Bacon, lard, or shallow frying in vegetable oil.
  • Use with caution: Flaxseed (flaking), extra-virgin olive oil (heavy smoke), coconut oil (soft layer).
  • Avoid for oven seasoning: Butter.

The best seasoning oil is the one you'll use thinly and consistently. Pick a cheap neutral oil, focus on technique, and let regular cooking do the rest.
