title: How to Season Cast Iron: A Step-by-Step Oven Method That Actually Works
description: A clear, tested process for seasoning a cast iron skillet in the oven, including oil choice, temperatures, how thin is thin enough, and how many coats you need.

Seasoning is the single most misunderstood part of owning cast iron. People hear that it takes years to "build up," or that you need a special oil, or that one wrong move will ruin it. None of that is true. Seasoning is a simple chemical process, and once you understand what is happening in the pan, you can do it well on the first try.

This guide walks through the oven method step by step, explains why each step matters, and covers the mistakes that cause sticky, blotchy, or flaking results.

## What seasoning actually is

Seasoning is not a coating of old grease. It is a thin layer of **polymerized oil**. When you heat a thin film of fat past its smoke point, the fat molecules break down and link together into a hard, plastic-like network that bonds to the iron. That layer is what makes the surface darker, smoother, and more resistant to rust and sticking.

Two things follow from this:

1. **The layer must be thin.** Polymerization happens where oil meets heat and air. A thick puddle of oil can't fully cure; the top hardens while the bottom stays gummy. That is where sticky pans come from.
2. **The heat must be high enough.** Below the oil's smoke point, oil just sits there and slowly goes rancid. Above it, it cures. That's why oven seasoning is done hot.

## What you need

- Your cast iron pan
- A neutral cooking oil or fat (vegetable oil, canola, shortening, or a commercial seasoning blend all work)
- Lint-free cloths or sturdy paper towels
- An oven
- Aluminum foil or a sheet pan (optional, for the lower rack)
- Dish soap, a scrubber, and a towel

You do not need flaxseed oil, a special wax, or an expensive product. More on oil choice in our [guide to the best oils for seasoning](/best-oil-for-seasoning-cast-iron/).

## Step 1: Start with a clean, dry pan

Wash the pan with warm water, a little dish soap, and a scrubber. Modern dish soap does not strip polymerized seasoning; it removes grease and food residue, which is exactly what you want gone before adding a new layer.

If the pan has rust, deal with that first. Seasoning over rust traps it and the new layer will flake. See [how to remove rust from cast iron](/how-to-remove-rust-from-cast-iron/).

Dry the pan with a towel, then put it on a burner over medium heat for two to three minutes, or in the oven while it preheats. Iron holds water in its pores and surface texture; heating drives it out. You should see any remaining moisture evaporate.

## Step 2: Preheat the oven

Set the oven to **450–500°F (230–260°C)**. Most common cooking oils have smoke points in the 400–450°F range, so this temperature gets them past the point where they polymerize. If your oven runs cool, lean toward the higher end.

Place a sheet of foil or a sheet pan on the lower rack to catch any drips.

## Step 3: Apply a very small amount of oil

While the pan is still warm (warm, not dangerously hot), add about half a teaspoon of oil for a 10–12 inch skillet. That's it. It will look like far too little.

Rub it over every surface: the cooking surface, the sides, the outside, the bottom, and the handle. Seasoning the whole pan protects it from rust everywhere, not just where you cook.

## Step 4: Wipe it all off

This is the step most people skip and the reason most people end up with sticky pans.

Take a clean cloth or paper towel and wipe the pan as if you made a mistake and are trying to remove all the oil. Buff it firmly. The pan should look dry, maybe with a faint sheen, but there should be no streaks, puddles, or visible wet spots. Whatever is left after this aggressive wipe is the right amount.

If you are unsure, wipe again with a fresh towel.

## Step 5: Bake it upside down

Place the pan **upside down** on the middle rack. Upside down lets any tiny excess oil drain instead of pooling in the bottom of the pan.

Bake for **one hour**.

There will be some smoke during the first part of the bake. That's the oil reaching and passing its smoke point, which is exactly what you want. Turn on the range hood and open a window.

## Step 6: Let it cool in the oven

Turn the oven off and leave the pan inside until it has cooled enough to handle, usually 30–60 minutes. Slow cooling is gentler on the iron and gives the layer time to finish setting.

## Step 7: Repeat three to five times

One coat creates a thin, often slightly brownish layer. Each additional coat deepens the color and evens out the surface. For a stripped or brand-new bare pan, **three to five coats** gives a durable base. For a pan that already has decent seasoning and just looks a little dull, one or two coats is plenty.

You can do the coats back-to-back on the same day. Oil the warm pan, wipe, bake, cool, repeat.

## What a good result looks like

After several coats, the pan should be:

- **Dry to the touch**, not tacky or sticky
- **Evenly colored**, ranging from dark bronze to black depending on the number of coats
- **Smooth-feeling**, with a semi-matte to satin sheen

Don't worry if it isn't jet black yet. Black, glossy seasoning comes from many layers, and cooking adds those layers naturally over time.

## Troubleshooting common problems

### The pan is sticky or tacky

Too much oil, too low a temperature, or not enough time. Put it back in a 450–500°F oven for another hour with no added oil. Often that's enough to finish curing the excess. If it's very gummy, scrub it with hot water and a stiff brush, dry it, and redo the coat with much less oil. See our full guide on [sticky and flaking seasoning](/sticky-or-flaking-cast-iron-seasoning/).

### There are brown or blotchy spots

Usually the result of uneven oil or only one coat. Keep adding thin coats; the blotches even out.

### There are drips or beads on the surface

Too much oil, and the pan wasn't upside down, or the oil migrated during baking. Scrub the beads down with a stiff brush or chain mail, then season again thinly.

### It's flaking off in black flakes

Flaking usually means the seasoning was applied too thick, applied over rust or old residue, or made with an oil that forms a brittle film. Flaxseed oil is a frequent culprit. Strip the loose areas and rebuild with thin coats of an ordinary oil.

## Do you need to oven season at all?

Not always. Most new cast iron, like Lodge pans, ships pre-seasoned and is ready to cook in. Oven seasoning is most useful when:

- You've stripped a pan to bare metal
- You've removed rust
- The seasoning is patchy, dull, or worn after heavy acidic cooking
- You've restored a vintage piece

For routine maintenance, simply cooking with fat and wiping a very thin layer of oil on the warm pan after washing keeps seasoning healthy. Searing, frying, and roasting with oil all add seasoning naturally.

## Stovetop seasoning: a quick alternative

If you want to touch up a pan without running the oven, you can season on the stovetop:

1. Heat the clean, dry pan over medium-high heat.
2. Add a few drops of oil and wipe it around with a folded paper towel held in tongs.
3. Wipe off the excess thoroughly.
4. Keep heating until the oil smokes and the surface goes from shiny to dry-looking, about 5–10 minutes.
5. Let it cool and repeat if desired.

The stovetop method only seasons the cooking surface well, and it's harder to get even, so the oven is better for full re-seasoning.

## Quick reference

| Step | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Clean | Wash, dry, heat dry | Removes residue and water |
| Preheat | 450–500°F | Gets oil past smoke point |
| Oil | ~½ tsp, all surfaces | Enough to coat |
| Wipe | Buff until it looks dry | Prevents sticky buildup |
| Bake | Upside down, 1 hour | Full polymerization |
| Cool | In oven | Gentle, complete cure |
| Repeat | 3–5 coats for bare iron | Durable base layer |

## The bottom line

Good seasoning comes down to three rules: thin oil, high heat, and repetition. If you remember nothing else, remember to wipe off far more oil than feels right. Do that and bake hot, and you will get a smooth, durable, nonstick-friendly surface that only gets better every time you cook.
