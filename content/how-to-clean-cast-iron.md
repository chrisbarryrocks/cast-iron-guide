title: How to Clean Cast Iron After Cooking (Yes, You Can Use Soap)
description: A practical daily routine for cleaning cast iron, what to do with stuck-on food, and the real story on soap, salt scrubs, soaking, and dishwashers.

Cleaning cast iron is easier than its reputation suggests. Most of the rules people repeat, like "never use soap" or "just wipe it out," come from a time when soap was made differently or from habits that were never necessary in the first place. Here's a simple routine that works for everyday cooking, plus how to handle the messy jobs.

## The short version

1. Clean the pan while it's still warm.
2. Rinse with hot water and scrub with a brush or scraper. A little dish soap is fine.
3. Dry it with a towel.
4. Heat it on the stove for a minute or two until fully dry.
5. Wipe on a few drops of oil and buff until it looks almost dry.

That's it. The rest of this article explains why and covers exceptions.

## The truth about soap

The "no soap" rule traces back to old lye-based soaps, which were harsh enough to damage seasoning. Modern dish soaps are mild detergents. They're designed to lift grease and food oils, not to break down the hard, polymerized layer that makes up seasoning.

Lodge, the largest US cast iron maker, says on its care pages that a little soap is fine to use. A small squirt of dish soap and a scrub will not strip a well-seasoned pan.

What *can* hurt seasoning:

- Long soaks in water
- The dishwasher, with its long hot cycles and harsh detergent
- Oven cleaner or lye-based products, which are used on purpose to strip seasoning
- Aggressive abrasives used hard and often on the cooking surface

If your seasoning comes off with normal dish soap, it wasn't fully cured to begin with. It was likely sticky, excess oil rather than polymerized seasoning.

## Step-by-step daily cleaning

### 1. Clean it warm

Food releases more easily from a warm pan. Let it cool enough to handle safely, but don't leave it sitting overnight. Food left in the pan holds moisture against the iron and can lead to rust spots.

Avoid running cold water into a very hot pan. Sudden temperature changes put stress on the iron and, in rare cases, can crack it. Warm pan, hot water is the safe combination.

### 2. Scrape out food

Use a flat-edged wooden or plastic scraper, or a metal spatula with a flat edge, to remove bits stuck to the bottom. A good spatula is one of the best cast iron tools you can own because it also smooths the surface as you cook.

### 3. Wash

Rinse with hot water and scrub with a stiff-bristled brush or a non-scratch sponge. Add a drop of dish soap if the pan is greasy. For stuck-on bits, see the sections below.

### 4. Dry thoroughly

Towel-dry right away. Then set the pan on a burner over medium heat for one to three minutes. This drives off moisture you can't see. You'll know it's dry when any damp sheen disappears.

### 5. Oil lightly

While it's warm, add a few drops of neutral oil and rub them over the cooking surface with a paper towel. Then wipe it again until the pan looks nearly dry. You're leaving a microscopic layer, not a coating. Too much oil left on the pan turns sticky and can go rancid in storage.

Some people let the pan heat a bit longer after oiling until it lightly smokes. That cures the thin layer and adds a tiny bit of seasoning. It's optional.

## Removing stuck-on food

### Simmer water

For stuck food, add an inch of water to the pan and bring it to a simmer for three to five minutes. Scrape with a spatula as it simmers. Most residue lifts right off. Pour out the water, then wash and dry as normal.

### Salt scrub

Pour a tablespoon or two of coarse kosher salt into the pan with a small splash of oil or water and scrub with a folded paper towel. Salt is abrasive enough to knock off residue but dissolves easily and doesn't scratch seasoning. Rinse it out, then dry and oil.

### Chain mail scrubber

A stainless chain mail scrubber is a popular tool for cast iron. It's very effective on stuck food and gentle on cured seasoning when used with water. It may remove loose, sticky, or poorly bonded seasoning, which is usually a good thing.

### Steel wool

Save steel wool for rust removal or when you plan to re-season. It can wear through seasoning if used aggressively on a regular basis.

## Cleaning after specific foods

**Eggs:** Usually wipe out easily. If stuck, simmer water briefly.

**Bacon and fried foods:** Pour off grease while warm (into a can or jar, not down the drain), wipe out the pan, and wash as needed. Bacon grease is great for maintenance, but a thick layer left on the pan turns sticky.

**Acidic foods like tomato sauce, wine, or vinegar:** Clean promptly. Long contact with acids can weaken seasoning and give food a metallic taste. Short cooking in a well-seasoned pan is fine; long simmers are better in enameled or stainless cookware.

**Fish or strong flavors:** Wash with soap and hot water to remove oils that carry odors. If an odor lingers, bake the dry pan at 400°F for 10–15 minutes.

**Sugary glazes and caramel:** Simmer water immediately to dissolve sugar before it hardens.

## Things to avoid

- **The dishwasher.** It strips seasoning and almost guarantees rust.
- **Soaking.** Even an hour or two of standing water can leave rust spots.
- **Air-drying.** Iron holds moisture; always towel and heat dry.
- **Storing with food in it.** Moisture and acids sit on the surface.
- **Putting a lid on while storing.** Trapped humidity encourages rust.

## Cleaning the outside and bottom

Grease and residue on the outside and bottom can build up and carbonize into a crusty, uneven layer. Scrub the outside occasionally with a brush and soap. If heavy buildup forms over time, it may be worth stripping and re-seasoning. See [how to restore vintage cast iron](/how-to-restore-vintage-cast-iron/) for stripping methods that also work on modern pans.

## Cleaning enameled cast iron

Enameled cast iron, like Dutch ovens with a colored outside and light inside, follows different rules. The enamel is a glass coating, so it does not need seasoning or oiling. Wash it with soap and water. For stains, simmer water with a spoonful of baking soda, or use a non-abrasive cleanser. Avoid metal scouring pads and sudden temperature changes, which can chip or crack enamel.

## How to tell your cleaning is working

A well-maintained pan looks evenly dark, feels smooth and dry, and releases food more easily over time. If the pan feels sticky, you're leaving too much oil. If you see dull gray or orange spots, it isn't drying fully or the seasoning is thin. Both are easy fixes, and our guides on [sticky seasoning](/sticky-or-flaking-cast-iron-seasoning/) and [rust removal](/how-to-remove-rust-from-cast-iron/) walk through them.

## Quick reference

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Everyday cleaning | Hot water, brush, a little soap, dry, heat, light oil |
| Stuck food | Simmer water 3–5 min and scrape |
| Light residue | Coarse salt scrub |
| Heavy stuck-on | Chain mail scrubber |
| Rust spots | Steel wool, then re-season |
| Odors | Soap wash, then dry-bake 10–15 min |

Treat cast iron like any other pan you care about, with one difference: dry it completely and give it a whisper of oil. Do that consistently and it will outlast you.
